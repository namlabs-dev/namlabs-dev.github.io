Box-line reduction

The mirror image of pointing pairs. If a row can only hold a digit inside one box, that box keeps its copy in that row, and the rest of the box can drop it.

What is box-line reduction?

Every row needs each digit once. Suppose that in one row, a digit can only go in cells that all lie inside the same box. Then the row's copy of the digit will be in that box, and that is the box's copy as well. So the digit cannot appear anywhere else in the box, which means you can erase it from the box's other two rows.

Pointing pairs work from the box towards the line. Box-line reduction works from the line towards the box. Same idea, opposite direction.

Example

Look at digit 4 in row 1. The right side of the row is already filled, and the 4 in R5C3 rules out R1C3. That leaves R1C1 and R1C2, both inside the top-left box.

So the 4 of row 1 is in that box, and the box already has its 4. Erase 4 from the box's other cells: R2C1, R2C2, R3C1 and R3C2. (R2C3 and R3C3 were already out because of the 4 in column 3.)

How to find it

  1. Pick a row (or column) and a digit it does not have yet.
  2. List the cells in that row where the digit can still go.
  3. If they all sit in one box, erase the digit from the other cells of that box.
Tip: if you found pointing pairs but the board is still stuck, flip the view: look along rows and columns instead of inside boxes.

Where it fits

Use it together with pointing pairs, and before the bigger patterns. When a digit's spots form a tidy rectangle across two lines, try X-Wing.
